# Using Film Engine

How to get a consistent-looking film out of it, in the order you actually do
the work. Written against the engine as it stands today.

---

## The one idea everything else follows from

An image model has no memory. Every frame you generate is generated by
something that has never seen any other frame in your film. Consistency is
therefore not something the model does — it is something **you attach to each
request**, and the whole of Film Engine is machinery for attaching the right
things without you having to remember them.

There are exactly four ways a fact reaches a generated frame:

| | What it carries | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **the style preset** | the look — grade, medium, era | the project, set once |
| **plates** | what a subject *is* — a face, a street, a car | generated or photographed per subject |
| **the anchor** | what a *scene* looks like, already rendered | a frame you point at |
| **the prompt** | what happens in this shot | the scene card |

Everything below is about feeding those four well. Anything not in one of them
does not reach the picture, however carefully you wrote it down.

## 0. Set up: quality, then providers

**Choose a quality, not a company.** Provider Settings leads with three tiers:

| Tier | What it is for | On Meshy credits |
|---|---|---|
| **Draft** | cheap variations while you are still deciding a composition | 3 credits |
| **Standard** | most storyboard frames | 3 credits |
| **Precision** | a frame that must hold an established location, a specific subject *and* a camera change at once | 9 credits |
| **Auto** | lifts to Precision by itself when a request carries several references or edits an existing frame | — |

Meshy proxies Google's Nano Banana models, so all three tiers run on Meshy
credits with no Google account. Google, Black Forest Labs (FLUX.2) and OpenAI
sit behind it as alternates; add a key for any of them and the tiers will
prefer it where it is better.

Each tier shows what it would *actually* use on your machine. If it says it is
falling through to something else, that is a missing key, and the card names
which one.

**Advanced** is where the provider dropdowns live, plus a model pin. Leave them
on Automatic unless you have learned that one specific model handles your
subject — an explicit pin always beats the tier, and the picker will tell you
when a pin is the thing deciding.

**Work in Draft while you are exploring.** The reason to have a cheap tier is
that exploration stops being a budget decision. Switch to Precision for the
frames you intend to keep.

## 1. Screenplay

Write or upload it. Then **create the entities from it** rather than typing them:
the Characters, Locations and Props pages each have *Describe from screenplay*,
which fills only what is blank.

This matters more than it looks. An entity with no description generates as a
bare name, and a bare name is re-invented from scratch in every frame it
appears in. That is where "the dragon is a different animal in each shot" comes
from — not from the image model being inconsistent, but from nothing having
been attached.

**Revising later:** editing the screenplay does *not* update the shots derived
from it. The board will tell you which scenes moved on without their shots, and
which shots are behind. Fix the card and the warning clears.

## 2. The look, before anything is generated

The **mood board** is where the look is decided. Pin references, write notes,
and press **Compose** to turn them into a style preset — then **Apply look** to
commit it to the project.

Compose is a preview; Apply is the commitment. Nothing changes until you apply.

The style preset is appended to *every* image prompt in the production. Two
consequences worth holding on to:

- **A style that names a subject will draw that subject in every frame.** A
  preset containing "anatomical beast" put a creature in an establishing shot
  whose card says "empty, ordinary, still". The engine warns you when it spots
  a subject word, but it will not block you — a creature film may genuinely
  want one.
- The board also carries **specs** — lens, sensor, stop, aspect ratio,
  resolution, frame rate, colour space. These reach previs, the image payload
  and the NLE export. A spec never overrides an explicit choice on a scene
  card; it fills in for shots that say nothing.

## 3. Plates: the single highest-leverage step

A plate is a reference picture of one subject. It is what makes MAYA the same
person in shot 12 as in shot 1.

- **Characters** get a three-view turnaround.
- **Locations and props** get an establishing or product plate.
- **Anything can be uploaded instead of generated** — a photograph of the real
  location, or the art department's character design. An upload lands exactly
  where a generation lands and is used identically.

**Declare sizes.** A plate is a close-up filling its own frame, and conditioning
transfers appearance, not scale — so a 30cm sprinkler plated at full frame comes
back the size of the car beside it. Put the real dimensions on the subject. The
**scale check** report lists every subject that has none, ordered by how many
shots it appears in.

### Location views

A location has *views*, not one plate. The default plate faces one way; a shot
pointing the other way needs a view of that direction, or it will be handed a
picture of what is behind the camera and will invent the rest.

- **Compass** generates east, south and west in one press, each one quarter-turn
  from the plate you already approved.
- The compass is a **convention, not a survey**: the existing plate *is* north,
  and the others follow by right-hand turns.
- A shot picks its view on the scene card (`location_view`), from a dropdown of
  what exists.
- **Refine** changes one thing about a plate — "the same street but wetter" —
  keeping the camera where it is. This is the operation to reach for when a
  plate is nearly right.

**Known limit on Meshy:** its adapter *edits* references rather than
conditioning on them, and an edit cannot move a camera. So a genuinely new view
is painted from the location's written description rather than turned from the
existing plate. The fuller the description, the closer the sides look. A direct
Google key does not have this limit.

### Lock what you have approved

Generating a plate does not make it binding. A **consistency profile**, once
**locked**, is what generation actually conditions on. Draft profiles reach
nothing — "I made a profile" and "the subject is now consistent" are different
statements.

## 4. Shots and scene cards

Break the screenplay down into scenes and shots, or tag lines directly into
shots from the screenplay view — the line *is* the description, so nothing is
paraphrased on the way.

The scene card is what every frame is generated from. Two fields, deliberately
separate:

- **description** — what the screenplay says. Revising the script replaces this.
- **direction** — what you add on top. Survives a rewrite.

Only the card reaches the prompt. Anything the screenplay says that the card
does not restate will not appear in the frame.

**Inserting a shot** between 2A and 2B gives you **2AA**, exactly as a script
supervisor would number it. Nothing is renumbered, because the codes are also
filenames.

## 5. Storyboard: the loop that costs money

### Read the prompt before you pay

Every paid action goes through a confirmation showing what will be sent — and
the free **prompt preview** shows the assembled prompt, the character ceiling,
the headroom, and **which pictures travel**. Read it. Most bad frames are
visible there before a credit is spent:

- a plate silently dropped for want of a reference slot,
- a camera note contradicting a staging line,
- language that names what it wants excluded.

That last one is worth internalising. **A diffusion model draws what you name.**
"We never see its face", "just off frame", "past camera" — these are screenplay
phrases, and every one of them tends to put the thing in the frame. The preview
flags them and suggests what to write instead.

### Direct this shot

One panel, both on the grid card and in the full-screen viewer:

- **Blocking** — who and what is in it, chosen from the project's own cast, so a
  typo cannot invent a subject with no plate. The list shows which subjects have
  no plate and will therefore be invented fresh.
- **Cinematography** — all eight camera facets, each showing what it would
  inherit if left blank.
- **Direction** — a free box for what you want on top of the writing.

### Refine, regenerate, recompose

Three different operations, and picking the wrong one wastes a generation:

| | Use when |
|---|---|
| **Regenerate** | the frame is wrong and you want another attempt from the card |
| **Refine** | the frame is right and you want to change *one thing*. Sends the picture plus one instruction — no card, no plates, no style, because the picture already carries them |
| **Recompose** | the performance and framing are right and the *place* is wrong. Sends two pictures: the frame whose acting is kept, and the plate whose location is adopted |

Refine cannot replace a background — its contract refuses composition changes,
and on a close-up the background *is* most of the composition. That is what
recompose is for.

### The anchor: consistency between shots

This is the tool for "the next shot should be the same street".

Point the anchor at a frame that has the scene right, and the following shots
are generated **from** it: same location, same dressing, subjects where they
stand — re-shot on whatever lens and angle that shot's own card asks for.

- Set it with ⚓ on a frame; setting another replaces it; clicking again clears it.
- One anchor per project, held deliberately. You pick it up while working on 1B
  and 1C, and put it down to go back to plates.
- It **replaces what it makes redundant** — the location plate stands down, and
  a subject the anchor's card names loses its long description (but keeps its
  name), which is what buys the prompt room back.
- **A close-up keeps its character plates anyway.** An anchor showing MAYA from
  behind carries her position and not her face. Close-up, extreme close-up,
  over-the-shoulder and insert all keep the plates automatically; 📌 in the
  Blocking panel forces one on any shot.
- Anchoring across scenes is allowed and reported — two scenes in one location
  is a good reason; two scenes in different locations is how you get the wrong
  street back.

If the confirmation says no anchor is attached and the scene ought to match, stop
and set it. A missing plate is subtle; a missing anchor changes the whole street.

### Versions

Every generation is a version and they are all kept. Choosing one is a
**pointer**, not a new generation — the count does not move and nothing is lost.
Each version has its own Refine, so you can work from an earlier attempt without
making the board show it.

**Send** copies a frame to another shot. It is always a copy: the source keeps
everything, the target gains a version, and the file is duplicated rather than
shared.

### Lock the board

When a board is finished, lock it. Every frame on it sits behind a button that
costs money and *replaces* the picture. Locking refuses all five of those paths
and stays overridable for the one frame that genuinely needs redoing. Editing
cards, reading and previewing stay free — "done" must not mean "frozen", or
nobody locks anything.

## 6. Previs: exploring angles for nothing

Previs is a 3D stage for the shot. Its point is that **trying an angle costs
nothing** — generating a candidate to find out is how trying three lenses
becomes a budget decision.

The toolbar runs in the order you use it:

**From card** → **Solve framing** → **Preview frame** → **Apply to card** → **Approve**

- *Preview frame* shows the prompt this blocking would generate and spends
  nothing.
- *Apply* writes the camera back onto the card. Until you apply, staging is an
  experiment — and the board will say so rather than quietly using it.
- *Approve* records the angle you signed off. Restage afterwards and generation
  refuses with a stale-approval warning rather than shipping a frame nobody
  approved.
- **Render this angle** saves, applies and regenerates in one press.

Blocking reaches both the still and the clip: staged positions become words the
image model can act on ("DRAGON in the near foreground at frame left, back to
camera"), and the camera path becomes motion in the video prompt.

Precedence is always **staged → written → what the production shoots on**, and
the board shows which source each facet came from.

## 7. Footage

**Preview before you generate.** The free video preview reports the provider,
model, length, size, the cost, and — the line that matters — **whether the
storyboard frame is attached**. A prompt can read perfectly while the frame that
would have made the clip match the board is missing, and then the footage comes
back a different place.

- **Runway gen4.5** takes two keyframes, first and last.
- **Seedance 2.5** takes up to thirty reference images on its omni-reference
  workflow, which is what makes "generate this specific sequence from these
  pictures" possible. Its resolution multiplies the price fivefold from 720p to
  4K, so it never rounds a resolution upward.

**Sequences** turn N chosen shots into N−1 segments, each travelling between two
frames you have already approved, then stitched into one file. Press order is
play order. A shot with no keyframe refuses the whole sequence rather than being
skipped — skipping silently joins the shots either side through a moment nobody
has seen.

**Clips made elsewhere can be uploaded** onto any shot. If one clip covers
several shots, say so on the shot card afterwards and playback, the conform and
all three NLE exports will treat it as one continuous piece rather than cutting
to stills partway through.

## 8. What is free and what is not

**Free:** reading anything, editing cards, all previews (prompt, video, refine,
recompose, previs), solving framings, sequence plans, cost estimates, stitching,
restoring a version, sending a frame to another shot.

**Costs money:** generating or regenerating a frame, refining, recomposing,
plates and compass sweeps, video, voice, music, 3D models.

Spend is metered per provider call and attributed to the project. The report
separates measured spend from reconstructed estimates — "we measured $41" and
"we think it was about $41" are different claims.

**LLM work is a subscription, not a bill.** Reasoning happens in the connected
agent host (Claude Desktop), so it charges the project nothing — but it consumes
your plan window, which is metered separately.

## 9. When something looks wrong

| Symptom | Usually means |
|---|---|
| a subject is different in every shot | it has no plate, or no locked profile |
| the street is wrong / rebuilt | no anchor attached, or the wrong location view |
| an object is the wrong size | no declared dimensions on the subject |
| the thing you excluded is in frame | the card names it — check the prompt preview's warnings |
| a plate looks like clip art | no style preset; the medium gets filled in for you |
| "generated work is behind what it was made from" | the inputs changed since it was generated. It does **not** mean the picture is wrong — accept them in one press if the work is still the film you want |
| the frame did not change after regenerating | it did; hard-refresh is no longer needed, but check the version list |

The two reports worth reading before a session: **staleness** (what is behind
its inputs) and **impact** (one change, all the way down — separating what to
redo now from what is waiting on something above it).
