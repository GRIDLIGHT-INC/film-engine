Pipeline and Flows

Two things that look similar on the menu and answer different questions. The Pipeline runs the film you have already planned. A Flow builds something the Pipeline does not do.

The distinction

One is a road. The other is a workshop.

The Pipeline is a fixed sequence of nine steps that every shot goes through, in the same order, every time. You do not design it and you cannot rearrange it — that is the point. It is the thing you run when the shots exist and you want footage out of them.

A Flow is a graph you draw yourself: boxes that generate something, wired together. You reach for one when you want a shot rendered by three different video models to compare, or a character sheet built four views at once, or any shape of work the nine steps do not have a slot for.

If you never touch Flows, you can still finish a film. If you only touch Flows, you will rebuild the Pipeline badly.

Pipeline

The nine steps, and what waits for what

Numbered because the order is real: each step names what it depends on, and the orchestrator will not start one before its inputs exist.

Depends on nothing.shot
02videoThe clip, generated from that frame.
Needs keyframe.shot
03voiceThe dialogue, spoken.
Depends on nothing.shot
04lipsyncMouth to the words.
Needs video + voice.shot
05musicThe scene's score.
Depends on nothing.scene
06sfxEffects written on the card.
Depends on nothing.shot
07ambientThe room the scene sits in.
Depends on nothing.scene
08postUpscale, grade, finish.
Needs lipsync.shot
09assemblyEverything into one clip.
Needs post · music · sfx · ambient.shot

Two of the nine are marked scene. A score and a room tone belong to a whole scene, not to one shot in it — so on a run covering several shots they are generated once per scene, not once per shot. That distinction is worth knowing because it is where money leaks: run them per shot and a three-shot scene buys three scores it will throw two of away.

Steps skip themselves when there is nothing to do. A shot with no dialogue does not run voice, and therefore does not run lipsync. A failed step retries up to three times with a growing gap before it gives up, because most provider failures are transient.

Where to start it

Run it onWhat happensUse it when
A shot The nine steps for that one shot. Scene-scoped steps are skipped unless you ask for them. Fixing or finishing a single shot.
A scene Every shot in the scene, with the score and room tone generated once. The normal unit of work. Start here.
The project Every scene, in order, sequentially. An overnight run once the board is signed off.

A run can be paused, resumed or cancelled while it is going. Pausing is the useful one: it stops before the next step rather than mid-generation, so you do not pay for work you then throw away.

Before you run it

The run plan is the part people miss

The Pipeline orders steps within one shot. It has no opinion about which shot to do first. The run plan does — and it is free, so there is no reason not to read it before a big run.

It skips work that is already current, reports what it skipped, and refuses outright if the projected cost is over the project's budget. Two orderings, and the trade is real:

OrderModel loadsWhat you get
By model Every model loads once Fastest overall. Nothing is finished until near the end.
By shot Models reload per shot A finished shot early, to look at. Slower overall.

On an eight-shot run those come out as 5 model switches against 47, for identical work at identical cost. Shot-major buys you something to watch after the first shot instead of after the last; that is the whole difference, and on a long run it is a real amount of time.

Flows

Twenty-three boxes and a wire

A flow is a graph. Each box takes typed inputs and produces typed outputs, and the canvas refuses a wire that would not make sense — you cannot feed text into a video input. There are four families:

in — where material comes from
  • prompt
  • asset
  • subject
  • scene
  • stock
gen — what costs money
  • image
  • video
  • voice
  • music
  • sfx
  • ambient
  • lipsync
  • post
  • model3d
  • llm
tf — shaping and branching
  • mix
  • stitch
  • encode
  • fanout
  • select
out — what is kept
  • asset
  • assembly
  • timeline

The two that make flows worth learning are tf.fanout and tf.select. Fan-out runs everything downstream of it once per branch — the same shot through three video models, or four views of one character. Select then pauses the run and waits for you to pick one. Nothing downstream of the gate spends until you have chosen.

Every gen box can also override the provider and model just for itself. That is all "multi-model" means here: one node pinned to a different generator than the project's default.

Six flows ship ready to run — prompt → image, multi-model video, character sheet, shot → clip, dialogue → lip-sync, and scene soundscape. There is also a built-in flow that mirrors the nine Pipeline steps exactly; it is read-only, and duplicating it is the sane way to start a custom graph.

Cost, before anything runs

A flow projects its cost before it generates, and multiplies rather than adds through nested fan-outs — a fan-out of four across a two-hundred-shot feature is eight hundred calls. If the projection is over budget the run is refused up front, not discovered on the ledger afterwards. You can override that deliberately; you cannot do it by accident.

In practice

A week, in the order it actually happens

  1. 01

    Get the board right first

    Screenplay, shots, plates, storyboard. Nothing below this is worth spending on until the frames are what you want — every later step is generated from them.

  2. 02

    Try the awkward shot as a Flow

    One or two shots in a film are unclear — a move nobody can picture, a look nobody agrees on. Fan out across models or prompts, put a select gate after it, and decide by looking. This is exploration, and it is the cheapest hour of the week.

  3. 03

    Read the run plan

    Free. It tells you what is already current, what it will cost, and refuses if you are over budget. Choose model order for an overnight run, shot order if you want something to watch by lunch.

  4. 04

    Run the Pipeline scene by scene

    The scene is the right unit: it is where score and room tone are generated once, and it is small enough that a bad result costs one scene rather than a film.

  5. 05

    Watch it, then conform and export

    Playback for the cut, then the master and the NLE handover. Finishing happens in the editor — the export carries dialogue, music, effects and ambience out on their own tracks so it can.

Traps

Six things that will cost you if nobody says them

scope

A single-shot run buys no score

Score and ambience are scene-scoped, so a run on one shot skips them deliberately — otherwise running five shots one at a time would buy five scores for one scene. If you genuinely want them, ask for scene steps explicitly.

order

Assembly is last for a reason

It waits on post, music, effects and ambience. If assembly is not running, something above it has not finished — read the run report rather than re-running the whole thing.

stale

It will refuse to build on something out of date

If a keyframe changed after the clip was made from it, the next step is refused rather than run. That refusal is the feature: generating from known-stale inputs spends money to produce something already known to be wrong. You can override it once you have looked.

flows

A generator box on its own keeps nothing

In a graph, saving is out.asset's job. Run a gen box with nothing downstream and the result comes back to you and reaches the project's asset list never — a paid generation that exists only in a transcript.

flows

The built-in flow cannot be edited

The one mirroring the nine steps is read-only on purpose, so it stays a true picture of what the Pipeline does. Duplicate it and edit the copy.

cost

Fan-outs multiply, they do not add

A fan-out inside a fan-out is the product of both. Read the estimate before running a graph you have just edited, every time — it is free, and it is the only warning you get.
